# Areas

Areas are the columns of a board. Each one is a stage of the work — Backlog, In
Progress, Done — and a card's area is its status. Moving a card is how work
progresses.

![A board with three areas and the panel for creating another](/images/docs/board-kanban.webp)

## Creating an area

Open a board and click **Create new area** at the right-hand end. Give it a name
and it appears as the next column.

Name areas after the state a card is *in*, not the action somebody takes:
"In Progress" tells you where the card is, "Start work" does not.

## Renaming and deleting

Click an area's name to rename it in place. The bin icon in its header deletes
it.

![The confirmation asked for before an area is archived](/images/docs/area-archive.webp)

Deleting an area deletes the cards in it, so move anything you want to keep to
another area first.

## Moving cards between areas

Drag a card and drop it where it belongs — another area, or another position in
the same one. It saves as you let go, and everyone else on the board sees it move
at the same moment.

## Columns or a list

A board can draw its areas two ways, and the switch is under **⋮ › Board
options › Style**:

**KanBan** stands the areas side by side. This is the one you want when work
moves through stages and you care about where things are piling up — a Backlog
with thirty cards and an In Progress with one tells you something at a glance.

![The same board in the ToDo style, with its areas stacked full width](/images/docs/board-todo.webp)

**ToDo** stacks the areas full width instead. This suits a board you simply work
down rather than move things across: a personal list, a checklist for a launch,
anything where the cards are not really travelling anywhere.

It is the same board and the same cards either way. The style is a property of
the board, so switching it changes nothing but the view.

## How many areas

Three or four is usually right. Every extra column is another decision about
where a card belongs, and a board wide enough to scroll sideways stops being
something you can take in at a glance — which is the whole point of a board.
